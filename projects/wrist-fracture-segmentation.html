🦴 Wrist Fracture AI Segmentation for Surgical Planning

September 2025 - January 2026 (18-week / half-year project)

Client: Specifix - AI-powered pre-operative planning for wrist fracture surgery

Team Members (Team Specifix): Daan Quaadvliet, Zsombor Szőcs, Anastasiia Mokhonko, Artjom Musaelans

Role: Team Lead & Data Scientist - Radius & Ulna Bone Segmentation

Project Overview

An 18-week research project with Specifix, a Belgian medtech startup building AI-powered pre-operative planning software for wrist fracture surgery. Our goal was to develop efficient AI models for automated radius and ulna bone segmentation from CT scans, directly addressing the 30-45 minute manual segmentation bottleneck that slows down surgical planning today. Wrist fractures account for up to 25% of pediatric fractures and 18% of elderly fractures globally, so faster, reliable segmentation has real clinical value.

The Challenge

Specifix's existing planning workflow relied on manual segmentation of radius and ulna bones from 3D CT scans, a slow and repetitive process that created a bottleneck ahead of surgery. The project needed to address:

• Building a reliable preprocessing pipeline for 162 real patient CT cases with inconsistent voxel spacing, intensity ranges, and file naming
• Selecting a segmentation architecture that balances clinical accuracy against computational cost, since Specifix wanted to minimize inference and training expenses
• Extending single-bone (radius) segmentation to a harder multiclass problem separating radius and ulna
• Navigating the regulatory reality of medical AI: EU MDR Class IIa classification, GDPR-compliant patient data handling, and EU AI Act high-risk system requirements
• Validating that any proposed solution would meaningfully reduce the surgeon's segmentation time without compromising safety

Our Approach

We treated this as a research-driven specialisation project, running a wide architecture search before converging on an efficient production candidate:

1. Data Engineering First: Built a multi-step preprocessing pipeline transforming fragmented, inconsistently-named NRRD files into standardized, model-ready patches
2. Baseline Before Custom: Established a state-of-the-art nnU-Net baseline before attempting any custom architecture, so every later comparison had a fair reference point
3. Broad Architecture Search: Systematically compared 10 architectures (3D U-Net, V-Net, Res-U-Net, nnU-Net and several custom variants) rather than committing early to one approach
4. Efficiency as a First-Class Metric: Tracked parameter count and GPU training hours alongside Dice score, since the client explicitly wanted to minimize compute cost
5. Multiclass Extension: Once single-bone segmentation was solid, extended the pipeline and models to jointly segment both the radius and ulna
6. Regulatory Awareness Throughout: Ran the legal/ethical analysis in parallel with model development instead of as an afterthought

Technical Implementation

The data pipeline transformed raw hospital CT exports into clean, patch-based training data through a series of dedicated stages:

Data Preprocessing Pipeline

1. Raw CT Scans - 162 patient cases (NRRD format), 1,499 training patches extracted in total
2. Data Cleaning - removed corrupted files and standardized fragmented exports
3. Label Merging - combined radius and ulna masks from separate source files
4. Voxel Spacing Standardization - resampled every scan to a consistent resolution
5. Intensity Normalization - normalized Hounsfield units to enhance bone tissue contrast
6. Train/Val/Test Split - patient-level split to avoid data leakage
7. Patch Extraction - 128×128×128 voxel patches (64³ for early iterations)
8. Intelligent Filtering - bone-content-aware sampling, ~90% background reduction
9. HDF5 Batching - packaged final patches for efficient GPU-side loading

    Comparing a state-of-the-art baseline against a purpose-built,
    lightweight architecture for radius bone segmentation.
    """
    def __init__(self):
        self.nnunet_baseline = {
            'architecture': 'nnU-Net (3D Full Resolution)',
            'dice_score': 0.90,
            'parameters': 31_000_000,
            'training_time_hours': 63
        }

        self.custom_resunet = {
            'architecture': 'Custom Res-U-Net',
            'dice_score': 0.869,
            'parameters': 3_200_000,       # ~90% fewer parameters
            'training_time_hours': 7       # ~89% less training time
        }

    def compare(self):
        param_reduction = 1 - (self.custom_resunet['parameters'] /
                                self.nnunet_baseline['parameters'])
        time_reduction = 1 - (self.custom_resunet['training_time_hours'] /
                               self.nnunet_baseline['training_time_hours'])
        dice_gap = self.nnunet_baseline['dice_score'] - self.custom_resunet['dice_score']

        return {
            'parameter_reduction': f"{param_reduction:.0%}",   # ~90%
            'time_reduction': f"{time_reduction:.0%}",         # ~89%
            'dice_gap': f"{dice_gap:.3f}"                      # ~0.031
        }

Regulatory & Ethical Framework

Because Specifix's software supports surgical decision-making, we treated legal and ethical compliance as a core deliverable rather than an afterthought:

EU AI Act: Classified the segmentation system as a high-risk medical AI application and worked through the associated transparency, human-oversight, and documentation obligations
EU MDR (Medical Device Regulation): Analyzed Class IIa certification requirements relevant to AI-assisted surgical planning tools
GDPR: Built data handling practices around anonymization, access control, and secure processing of patient CT data
Bias & Fairness: Assessed potential bias from demographic representation and scanner variability in the training data, and proposed stratified validation as a mitigation
Explainability: Used 3D Slicer visualizations and multi-metric evaluation (Dice, IoU, Hausdorff distance) so predictions remain interpretable to clinical stakeholders, not just a black-box output

Key Learnings

Efficiency Is a Design Constraint, Not an Afterthought: Treating parameter count and training time as first-class metrics alongside Dice score, rather than optimizing for accuracy alone, led directly to a model that was actually usable within the client's compute budget.

Validate Loss Functions Against Real Predictions: A late-stage bug where a multiclass model was accidentally trained with the wrong loss function caused it to silently collapse into predicting only "bone" instead of distinguishing radius from ulna. Good validation scores alone did not catch this - only full-volume inference did. This reinforced the importance of sanity-checking predictions directly, not just trusting aggregate metrics.

Establish a Baseline Before Getting Creative: Running the nnU-Net baseline first, even though it was expensive, gave every later architecture experiment a fair and credible point of comparison.

Medical AI Changes the Rules: Working with real patient CT data made regulatory and ethical considerations (EU MDR, GDPR, EU AI Act) a genuine part of the technical workflow, not a separate compliance exercise bolted on at the end.

Log Everything, Immediately: Maintaining a running model log across 150+ experiments (architecture, hyperparameters, Dice scores, training time, and notes) was essential to keep the architecture search comparable and to avoid losing results that couldn't easily be reproduced.

Academic Research Paper

Here's our technical research report, "AI-Based Radius and Ulna Bone Segmentation for Wrist Fracture Surgical Planning," covering the full methodology, benchmark results, and regulatory analysis behind the project:

AI-Based Radius and Ulna Bone Segmentation for Wrist Fracture Surgical Planning

Authors: Thijn Bakker, Daan Quaadvliet, Zsombor Szőcs, Anastasiia Mokhonko, Artjom Musaelans
Supervisors: Arash Sadeghzadeh (Academic Supervisor, BUas), Dr. Soha Mahdi (Industry Supervisor, Co-founder & CTO, Specifix B.V.)
Institution: Breda University of Applied Sciences, in collaboration with Specifix B.V.
Date: January 21, 2026

Abstract

This report documents the development and validation of efficient custom deep learning architectures for automated radius and ulna bone segmentation from CT scans, built to support pre-operative planning for distal radius fractures. The work responds to a real computational bottleneck: Specifix's existing nnU-Net-based workflow delivers strong accuracy but at a compute cost that slows down iteration and deployment. We trained nnU-Net ourselves to establish a rigorous benchmark, then developed custom ResU-Net architectures that approach its clinical accuracy with a fraction of the resources.

Keywords: Medical Image Segmentation, Deep Learning, nnU-Net, ResU-Net, Computational Efficiency, Wrist Fracture, EU MDR

1. Clinical Background & Problem Definition

Distal radius fractures are among the most common orthopedic injuries worldwide, accounting for roughly 8-18% of all fractures, rising to as much as 25% of pediatric injuries and 18% of fractures in elderly patients. Surgeons currently spend 30-45 minutes per case manually segmenting the radius (and often the ulna) from CT scans before surgical planning can begin.

Key Challenges Identified
  • Manual segmentation creates treatment delays, resource misallocation, and inter-operator variability in surgical planning quality
  • Fractured anatomy can break the radius into multiple displaced fragments that still need to be recognized as one structure
  • The radius and ulna share very similar CT density characteristics, making them easy to confuse algorithmically
  • The available dataset (162 CT cases from Specifix, via Radboud University Medical Center) is small by deep-learning standards

Specifix currently relies on nnU-Net, a state-of-the-art but computationally expensive framework. The project's goal was to establish nnU-Net as a rigorous benchmark, then design purpose-built architectures that could approach its accuracy while dramatically cutting parameter count, training time, and inference time.

2. Data Preparation

The raw dataset arrived as fragmented NRRD files (162 cases, expanded from an initial 114 by treating contralateral healthy scans as independent cases) and required an 8-step preprocessing pipeline: cleaning and case restructuring, label merging and naming standardization, voxel spacing standardization to 0.6mm isotropic resolution, Hounsfield-unit intensity normalization, a stratified 70/15/15 train/validation/test split, 128×128×128 voxel patch extraction, intelligent bone-aware patch filtering, and HDF5 batching for efficient GPU loading.

3. Research Methodology

The team ran a systematic architecture search: over 150 training runs across 3D U-Net, ResU-Net, V-Net, MultiScale ResU-Net, and a self-configured nnU-Net benchmark. Every architecture was evaluated under identical preprocessing, identical splits, and consistent logging so results were directly comparable. Evaluation combined patch-level Dice scoring (matching the training distribution) with full-volume inference (matching real deployment), plus sliding-window inference with Gaussian weighting and post-processing (largest-component selection, hole filling, morphological closing) to keep predictions clinically usable.

4. Results: Benchmark vs. Custom Architectures

The nnU-Net benchmark reached 90.0% test Dice (post-processed) with 31M parameters, 63 hours of training, and roughly 6.5 minutes of inference per case - confirming both the feasibility of the task and the computational cost the project set out to reduce.

  • nnU-Net 3D: 31.0M params · 90.0% Dice · 63.0h training
  • ResU-Net v4 (24f): 3.2M params · 86.9% Dice · 7.0h training · <4 min inference
  • ResU-Net v5 (20f): 2.2M params · 86.2% Dice · 8.6h training
  • V-Net-Res: 11.6M params · 82.4% Dice · 12.5h training
  • 3D U-Net (32f): 12.9M params · 77.6% Dice · 2.7h training

ResU-Net v4 became the recommended production candidate: roughly a 90% reduction in parameters and an 89% reduction in training time for about a 3-point Dice trade-off against the nnU-Net benchmark. 18 of 23 test cases (78.3%) met the clinical threshold of DSC ≥ 0.85.

Extending to multiclass radius+ulna segmentation, the best model reached 80.7% mean Dice (radius 75.7%, ulna 85.7%) using only 2.9M parameters and 3 hours of training - ulna consistently segmented more easily than radius, likely due to its larger, more consistent anatomy. A notable finding across both tasks: the best-performing models were trained without data augmentation, suggesting the preprocessing pipeline already provided sufficient consistency.

5. Regulatory & Ethical Framework

Because the system supports surgical decision-making, it is classified as a high-risk AI system under EU AI Act Article 6.1 and as a Class IIa medical device under MDR Rule 11 (Annex VIII) - triggering requirements for risk management, technical documentation, transparency, human oversight, and accuracy validation, plus CE marking and ISO 13485-compliant quality management for the MDR side.

The ethical analysis drew on four complementary frameworks: the UNESCO Recommendation on Ethics of AI, the EU High-Level Expert Group Guidelines on trustworthy AI, WHO standards for medical AI, and WMA guidance on physicians' professional duties. Risk assessment covered segmentation accuracy, automation bias, privacy/security, fairness (given known demographic gaps in the dataset), and edge-case failures - each paired with a concrete mitigation such as mandatory surgeon review, manual correction tools, and encrypted data storage.

The resulting human-AI collaboration model keeps a surgeon in the loop for every case: automated outputs are always reviewed, correctable, and require explicit approval before use in planning.

6. Conclusions & Recommendations

The project met its central objective: custom architectures (ResU-Net v4/v5) reached 86.2-86.9% test Dice, above the 85% clinical acceptability threshold and within 3.1-3.8 points of the nnU-Net benchmark, while cutting parameters by 90-93% and training time by 86-89%. Every model kept full-case processing under 10 minutes, an 85-90% improvement over the 30-45 minute manual workflow.

Deployment Recommendation

Deploy ResU-Net v4 for routine, cost-sensitive segmentation; reserve the nnU-Net benchmark for complex cases where the extra ~3 points of accuracy are worth the compute cost. A hybrid strategy - routing by case complexity or model confidence - was proposed as a practical middle ground.

Next Steps
  • Expand the dataset toward 1,000+ multi-center cases with demographic documentation to support fairness validation
  • Consolidate the risk management file into ISO 14971 format and pursue early notified-body engagement for CE marking
  • Build structured user training covering common AI error patterns and appropriate escalation to manual review
  • Explore transfer of the pipeline to other orthopedic structures (carpal bones, ankle, spine) and adjacent tasks like fracture classification
